// architecture

Fifteen apps don't get built fifteen times.

The reason this volume is possible at all is that almost none of it is written twice. What follows is the part I'd actually want to talk about in an interview.

01

Shared code lives in packages

Anything more than one app needs — networking, auth and token refresh, navigation scaffolding, form and list widgets, whole feature modules like the financial flows — sits in internal packages rather than being copied between projects. An app consumes what it needs and adds only what makes it that product.

02

Theming stays anonymous

No package knows what colour it is. Each app declares its own scheme, typography, and assets; shared widgets resolve everything through the theme they're handed at runtime. That's why seven branded apps built from one codebase don't look related — and why a new brand is a configuration, not a fork.

03

Localization built in from the start

Strings live with the packages that own them, so adding a language is a translation task rather than a code change. Arabic means the layout has to mirror as well as translate, so the shared widgets were written direction-aware rather than retrofitted later.

04

Dependencies point inward

Shared pages don't reach for an app's specific state management. They declare what they need as a contract, and each app injects its own implementation through GetIt. That's what keeps a package reusable instead of quietly coupling itself to whichever app it was born in.

// how the work happens

From a stakeholder conversation to a store listing.

I own more of this cycle than a typical mobile role. It runs end to end, and the last step feeds back into the middle.

  1. Gather the requirement

    Sit with the stakeholder — internally at Technia, and often directly with the client for real estate work. Understand the business need before the feature request, because what people ask for and what they need are frequently different things.

  2. Turn it into structured work

    Translate the conversation into concrete development tasks and a technical approach, and agree the API contract with the backend team so the shape of the data matches the shape of the screen.

  3. Design the UI/UX

    Design the screens myself, using AI-assisted workflows and research into how comparable products solve the same problem. The design adapts to the business model — a B2B admin tool and a B2C member app get different treatment.

  4. Build it

    Implement against the shared packages, adding to them when the feature is general and keeping it in the app when it isn't. That judgement call is most of what keeps the suite maintainable.

  5. Launch

    Through review and out to both stores — Google Play and the App Store, including the store-side work that isn't code.

  6. Back to the client, and around again

    Take bug reports and enhancement requests from the people actually using it, triage them against the roadmap, and feed them back into the cycle. Shipping is where the conversation restarts, not where it ends.

// domain fluency

You can't build accounting software you don't understand.

Enterprise apps fail on domain misunderstandings more often than on technical ones. Building across these areas meant learning the vocabulary and the processes properly, so that a stakeholder conversation doesn't need translating first.

Sales

How a lead becomes an account, where a quotation sits relative to an RFQ, how work orders and subscriptions carry through, and why a representative's pipeline view differs from a manager's.

Accounting

Invoices against bills, credit notes against debit notes, customer and vendor accounts, and the difference between recording a transaction and reporting on it.

Purchasing

Purchase requests against purchase orders, supplier records, approval chains and who can release what, and where procurement touches inventory.

HR

Attendance and clocking rules, leave and request workflows, payroll visibility, and why the employee-facing app and the admin-facing app are two products rather than two permission levels.

Real estate

Lead qualification, follow-up cadence, action logging, and how deals move through stages — gathered largely from client meetings rather than from a spec.

Fitness

Packages against memberships against subscriptions, session counting, coach scheduling, and gate access — the operational reality of running a gym.
